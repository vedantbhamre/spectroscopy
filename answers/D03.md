## Customer enquiry

> Our dairy process includes a relatively clear whey stream before a concentration stage. We want to investigate whether spectroscopy can track protein concentration as the stream changes, with laboratory results used as the reference. The stream can become more turbid under some operating conditions. We need a first measurement concept that handles this change rather than assuming the liquid always remains clear.

- **NIR (diffuse reflection)** (comparison): Diffuse‑reflection NIR probes are recommended for scattering media and can continue to give usable spectra when the whey becomes turbid; they are also average‑cost and can share the same spectrometer. [S01 Table 1 (p.6)]

## Measurement concept

Install a transmission NIR probe in a low‑OD section of the line for baseline protein monitoring; add a diffuse‑reflection NIR probe downstream where turbidity may increase. Both probes connect via a single low‑OH chalcogenide fiber to a bench‑top NIR spectrometer with chemometric calibration against lab‑measured whey samples. [S01 p.5; S01 p.17; S01 p.18]

- **Analyzer:** Bench‑top NIR spectrometer (780–2500 nm) with chemometrics software
- **Fibre link:** Low‑OH silica or chalcogenide fiber (compatible with 780–2500 nm)
- **Probe:** Transmission NIR probe (liquid) and diffuse‑reflection NIR probe (scattering)
- **Process point:** Transmission probe in the clear‑flow section; reflection probe in the high‑turbidity section (flush‑mounted in pipe)

## Spectral range

NIR absorption range 780–2500 nm (transmission) and the same range for diffuse‑reflection; absorption not applicable. [S01 p.10]

## Risks and uncertainty

- Transmission probe may saturate or lose signal if turbidity exceeds the path‑length limit.
- Diffuse‑reflection probe may be surface‑biased and less representative of bulk protein when particles settle.
- Calibration drift if temperature or flow conditions change significantly without compensation.

## First feasibility experiment

1. Collect whey samples covering the full turbidity range (clear to worst‑case scattering).
2. Measure each sample with the lab reference method (e.g., Kjeldahl or BCA assay) for protein.
3. Acquire NIR transmission spectra on the clear‑flow probe and diffuse‑reflection spectra on the turbid‑flow probe.
4. Develop separate chemometric calibration models (e.g., PLS) for each probe using the reference data.
5. Validate each model on held‑out samples, including intermediate turbidity levels, to confirm robustness.
6. Then measure in-line or in a pilot set-up against the reference method.

**Decision basis:** If both models meet the agreed‑upon validation criteria (e.g., RMSEP within the laboratory method’s uncertainty), the dual‑probe concept is feasible. Agree two things separately with the customer before the test: the model accuracy required against the reference method, and the alarm or action threshold applied to the measured value.

## Assumptions

- Protein has characteristic NIR overtones that can be modeled with chemometrics.
- The spectrometer can be shared between the two probes without re‑alignment.
- Laboratory reference measurements are accurate and can serve as calibration truth.
